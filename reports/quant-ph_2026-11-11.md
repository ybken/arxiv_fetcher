### Abstract

Hardware executions of the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) are often validated by comparing the device's mean cost with a simulation, a check that can miss what matters for optimization. Under global depolarization, the mean normalized gap and the floor-corrected probability of every set of bitstrings, the optimum included, retain the same fraction of their noiseless improvement over uniform sampling. We test this prediction with depth-2, transferred-angle QAOA circuits for binary subproblems of an alternating-direction decomposition on the IBM Heron processor \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh}, with and without dynamical decoupling and twirling. At 12 qubits, the main-family circuits retained 29\% to 46\% of their mean-gap improvement but only 5\% to 15\% of their probability of the optimum, lower on at least nine of ten instances per run. Near-optimal bitstrings were lost about as much as the optimum, whereas the avoidance of poor bitstrings was retained best. In the first submission, a device-matched noise model reproduced the pattern but overestimated the retention of the optimum five- to eightfold. On a rugged planted family, the device found the optimum in 62\% of 768-shot blocks (noiseless: 98\%), within the range of local heuristics. Validations of optimization circuits should report retention of the optimum and of near-optimal sets.

### Abstract

Fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states support fractionalized excitations and geometric phases, yet experimentally resolving the many-body coherence encoded in their wave functions remains challenging. **Authors:** Yakir Aharonov, Avshalom C. Elitzur

**Link:** http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12971v1

**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.12971v1

**Submitted:** 2026-11-07

### Abstract

A quantum system prepared in a superposition of proper times - a clock whose worldline is superposed, or a clock at rest in a superposed gravitational potential - evolves, in each branch, by the same dynamics for a different duration. Post-selecting the control that sets the proper time turns the superposition into a superoscillation of the time-dilation factor: the system is found to have evolved for a proper time $α_w t$, where the weak value $α_w$ may exceed unity, vanish, or be negative - the quantum time-translation machine of Aharonov, Anandan, Popescu and Vaidman. We ask how far back such a machine can run an irreversible process. The test system is a sealed cell - excited atoms, the radiation field they emit into, and the absorbing walls, carried as one quantum object - and the witness is projective: atoms that had begun to decay before the run are found, in the post-selected sub-ensemble, more excited than when inserted. The answer is exact and depends only on the atoms' survival amplitude. With two branches the witness holds only while the decay is younger than the memory time of the environment, after which the emission becomes Markovian. Beyond it, the post-selection amplifies the minute difference between the contents of the cell in the two branches, set by the full energy spread of the atoms and their field rather than by the linewidth, and the witness fails. Reversing a decay past the memory time requires many branches, an environment with a strictly bounded spectrum, and a post-selection probability that falls exponentially with the number of environmental oscillations to be undone. The obstacle to reversal is therefore not entropy but bandwidth - the same bandwidth that makes the emission irreversible in the first place. We give four ways to superpose a clock rate, from hollow massive shells to inertial velocities.

### Abstract

Artificial spin arrays assembled on surfaces enable atomic-scale studies of quantum correlations. Here we establish a hierarchy of connections between spin inelastic electron tunneling spectroscopy (IETS) and entanglement in spin-$1/2$ systems. We first show that the number of spectroscopically active transitions in a spin-$1/2$ dimer serves as an entanglement witness. Using the leading-order tunneling conductance for the tip placed on spin $i$, $dI(i)/dV$, we then relate spectral moments $\mathcal{M}^{(n)}(i)=\int V^n(d^2I(i)/dV^2)\,dV$ to quantum correlations. The zeroth moment probes the one-tangle in systems with a gapped, nondegenerate ground state. For rotationally invariant Heisenberg systems, the first moment yields thermal spin correlators $\langle\boldsymbol S(i)\cdot\boldsymbol S(j)\rangle$, enabling reconstruction of nearest-neighbor concurrence and spatial imaging of entanglement in spin chains. It also provides access to the internal energy and magnetic specific heat, both usable as entanglement witnesses. Applying this approach to experimental IETS data from nanographene spin chains, we find good agreement with theory. We further discuss how the second moment probes longer-range correlations and the inverse moment relates to local spin susceptibility. For spin-$1/2$ trimers, we connect inter-multiplet inelastic spectral weight to genuine tripartite entanglement quantified by the concurrence fill. Finally, we reconcile the local character of IETS with its sensitivity to nonlocal entanglement through an analogy with digital quantum circuits, where Hamiltonian evolution implements multiqubit operations. These results establish IETS as a tool for quantifying and imaging entanglement in engineered on-surface spin systems.

### Abstract

Whether gravity must be quantum is often approached by asking whether two masses can become entangled through their mutual gravitational interaction. ### Abstract

Simulating a bosonic degree of freedom on qubit hardware requires discretizing its position space, and the qubit encoding of the resulting lattice determines the circuits that realize its real-time dynamics. We compare binary and unary encodings of a position lattice on superconducting (IBM) and trapped-ion (IonQ) processors. Coupled-oscillator benchmarks, where inter-register interactions prevent this exact compression, show that the preferred encoding depends on the grid size and the evolution time.
