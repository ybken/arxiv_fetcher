**Authors:** Teerawat Tongloy, Rangsan Jomtarak

**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.01993v2

**Submitted:** 2026-11-01

### Abstract

When an image classifier decides, we can usually only estimate why. For quantum and all-optical classifiers that act linearly on amplitude-encoded pictures up to the measurement, with no trained classical network before or after them, each class's Born probability above its no-information share, $P_g - b_g$, splits exactly into contributions of the encoded amplitudes, shown as Born evidence maps (BEM) over the picture. The split itself follows from known results on quadratic forms: with the transmitted light held fixed, it is what Integrated Gradients from a zero baseline and the Shapley value give. What we add is a way to measure it on the hardware that classifies, from class probabilities on a quantum chip or from camera frames on a lens bench, with no gradient circuit and no light sent backwards; a $g\times g$ map takes $1 + 2g^2$ runs. Deleting the 5% of pixels with the highest evidence drops accuracy from 73% to 9%, as occlusion does with 1,024 extra runs per picture, whereas gradient saliency removes much less. The classifiers share one architecture, a Fourier-plane mask followed by a brickwall circuit on qubits or by phase plates on a lens bench, read by a fixed Born headroom readout with no trained parameter; centring each picture on its own mean lets dark features count as much as bright ones. In simulation, four phase plates at a single wavelength reach 72.3% on Caltech-10 colour photographs. On IBM's ibm_kingston, with the mask applied before approximate loading, 300 test pictures were classified at 37.7% against 10% by chance (56.3% in exact simulation), and $2\times2$ maps of ten correctly classified pictures, one per class chosen by a stated rule, measured on the chip, agreed with the exact ones ($r = 0.77$). The maps also revealed a shortcut in the data; as the trim that removed it was chosen on test pictures, the Caltech-10 results are exploratory.

### Abstract

Ground states of Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK)-type models are volume-law entangled, which makes them costly both for tensor networks and for quantum hardware simulations. Thus, it remains unclear where quantum hardware could outperform classical simulation of such models. In this work, we study a spin SYK-dot chain, in which q dense SYK dots of p qubits are joined by single ZZ bonds of strength K. The entanglement across any cut is then bounded by floor(p/2) ln 2 plus the inter-dot entanglement, and the two-qubit depth of one Trotter step is set by p alone. A resource estimate shows that a quantum advantage for the ground state could appear only for dots of about 15 to 21 qubits or more. There, a single Trotter step already requires two-qubit error rates of order 10^{-6}, beyond the reach of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. We test the assumptions of this estimate with an imaginary-time matrix product state (MPS) that applies each dot as one block. It agrees with exact diagonalisation at (p, q) = (6, 2). At (5, 2), a variational circuit with the same dot-plus-link layout comes within 3.6% of the exact energy, and noisy simulations show that its fidelity decays as assumed in the resource estimate. At (8, 5), i.e., N = 40 qubits, the energy converges at bond dimension chi = 768, far below the exact value 2^20, and the inter-dot entanglement stays small and is set mainly by K. Out-of-time-order correlators across the links decay on a time scale that shrinks rapidly with |K|. Thus, the dot size p and the inter-dot coupling K together set the entanglement of the chain, and with it where a quantum advantage could appear.

### Abstract

Continuous blind stabilizer measurements turn stabilizer codes into a Liouvillian spectral problem in which Hamiltonian terms compete with dissipative error channels. We show that this competition generically enables Liouvillian exceptional points, whose occurrence at weak error rates is constrained by the code distance. Exceptional points are detrimental in this regime of error correction as they maximize the decay rate of encoded coherences. We then optimize the encoding of the logical qubit using the eigenmodes of the Lindblad operator. This phase-optimized encoding cancels the linear short-time decay of logical qubit coherences. We apply this encoding to the [[5,1,3]] code and demonstrate that it outperforms naive encoding methods near the exceptional point, improves the fidelity of a logical $π$ rotation and remains effective in the presence of additional weak error channels.

### Abstract

The Kramers theorem generally guarantees the spectral degeneracy of time-reversal-invariant Hamiltonian operators with half-integer spin, underlying a variety of physical phenomena. Despite its fundamental significance, the Kramers degeneracy has been argued to be absent in Liouvillian superoperators acting on mixed states because of the double structure of the Hilbert space. ### Abstract

The complete statistical characterization of defects generated in a quantum phase transition has mostly been studied in exactly solvable models, and remains little explored in nonintegrable systems such as long-range interacting spin chains. We characterize the full defect counting statistics in a nonintegrable long-range spin chain, the fractional Ising model, by time-dependent variational-principle simulations within the matrix-product-state and matrix-product-operator formalism. Fractional interactions capture emergent multiscale correlations and long-range effects in nature, governed by the order of the underlying fractional derivative. Such couplings can be approximated on existing quantum simulation platforms, where the full defect statistics can be measured. The defect number cumulants follow power laws in the driving time, with exponents that vary continuously with the fractional order. At low fractional order, deep in the long-range regime, the mean defect number decays more slowly than predicted by the Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM), while at intermediate order the KZM holds with the numerically extracted many-body critical exponents. At large fractional order, the exactly solvable free-fermion limit of the model becomes progressively more accurate towards the short-range limit. We derive exact bounds on the kink-number cumulants and on the kink-kink correlations, implying an exact Gaussian limiting distribution for large system sizes. In the many-body simulations, however, kinks are more correlated, consistent with the confinement of domain walls, and these bounds are violated in the long-range regime, also in the bulk of the chain. Nevertheless, the kink distribution still approaches a Gaussian for large defect numbers, with proportionality partially broken between the cumulants.

### Abstract

### Abstract

We propose a physical generative model for learning correlated multivariate data based on homodyne detection of photonic continuous-variable (CV) circuits. We use an $N$-mode photonic circuit in which two-mode squeezing provides correlations, local phase shifts tune the strength and sign of these correlations, and an adjustable interferometer redistributes them across modes. We train the circuit by matching its Pearson correlation matrix to the one obtained from data. We apply affine post-processing to adjust the marginals independently while keeping the relations intact. We test the model on the multivariate distribution of standardized log returns of financial assets, showing that the photonic model can represent rich correlation structures. We reproduce empirical correlations with residuals below $10^{-4}$ for five variables and recover the correct correlation signs for $94\% \pm 1\%$ of 190 correlation pairs at twenty variables, with $86\%$ of the residuals below $0.1$. As a separate technical contribution, we develop an approach for simulating Kerr gates with small nonlinearity via a Hubbard-Stratonovich expansion, making them suitable for CV-based simulators. Our results establish CV photonic circuits as a platform for interpretable generative learning, suitable for representing and fast sampling of distributions across different application domains.

In the graph collision problem, the task is to decide whether a known graph on $n$ vertices contains an edge with both endpoints marked, given query access to the input bits specifying the marked vertices. ### Abstract

The Pointwise Dimension is a diagnostic that reveals the effective dimensionality of classical trajectories in a $2N$-dimensional phase space. The essence of this diagnostic is to find a scaling according to which density changes with radius $r$ around a point of interest in phase space. Particularly, regular trajectories are confined to an $N$-dimensional invariant torus, while chaotic trajectories explore a $(2N-1)$-dimensional energy surface. In classical mechanics, this trajectory density can be found by counting recurrences to within the sphere of radius $r$. In quantum mechanics, probability density is intrinsic, making the Pointwise Dimension compelling, but surprisingly this has not been studied in the quantum regime. In this work, we implement the Pointwise Dimension in the quantum regime using the Husimi representation of the quantum wavepacket. This implementation entails a long-time average of the time-dependent Husimi representation to obtain a stationary phase space density, and a radial spatial integration of this density over a sphere of radius $r$. The classical-quantum correspondence is excellent for both autonomous and non-autonomous systems, adding a new diagnostic to the study of quantum chaos. Finally, we explore the possibility, both classically and quantum mechanically, of obtaining the Pointwise Dimension from a time correlation function.

### Abstract

We investigate quantum error correction protocols for topological codes deformed away from their stabilizer limits, particularly as they are pushed towards quantum phase transitions into trivial phases. Our focus is primarily on quantum information stored within the ground-state manifold of the transverse-field toric code (TFTC), subjected to independent bit-flip and phase-flip decoherence. Although the transverse field induces strong quantum fluctuations of $m$ anyons in the ground states, we show that projective measurements of the "bare" toric code stabilizers can nevertheless be used to construct an efficient decoding algorithm which exhibits a finite threshold to both types of decoherence, even when the transverse field is tuned infinitesimally close to the quantum critical point. Our construction of the optimal postmeasurement decoders---that is, the optimal decoders assuming that the stabilizers are first measured---crucially relies on the stoquastic nature of the TFTC Hamiltonian, which affords a description of its ground states in terms of a classical lattice gauge theory. Using stoquasticity, we first prove that stabilizer measurements act unitarily on the undecohered TFTC ground states to excellent approximation, and that the optimal postmeasurement decoder can be efficiently implemented via Monte Carlo simulation of a constrained lattice gauge theory. We then construct the optimal postmeasurement decoder for the TFTC states under bit-flip and phase-flip decoherence, where we numerically demonstrate a finite threshold close to the quantum critical point. We provide theoretical evidence (without a rigorous proof) that stabilizer measurements constitute an optimal decoding strategy in this setting. For phase-flip decoherence, we further prove that the optimal postmeasurement decoding threshold is independent of the transverse field strength throughout the topological phase.
