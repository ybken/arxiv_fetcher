### Abstract

Ultra-high-rate quantum error correction codes store many logical qubits in a single code block with few physical qubits. A fault-tolerant quantum computer, however, also requires a universal set of logical operations on individually addressable logical qubits, and the ancilla overhead of these operations may erode the advantage of the high encoding rate. Here, we propose the Cornucopia architecture, in which a universal set of logical operations is implemented at low overhead for the Cornucopia codes. In particular, we focus on the [[2844,1426,18]] Cornucopia code as an example and introduce a modular quantum computer built from such code blocks connected by bridges. Each block stores 1,426 logical qubits and serves as a universal logical processor. Exploiting the code's translation symmetry, we construct a family of reusable gadgets that individually address 1,422 logical qubits within each block. These gadgets support three universal logical instruction sets: basic, high-throughput and fixed-extractor. Excluding magic-state factories, a module with sufficient workspace to execute any instruction in the basic set requires an average of 4.1 physical qubits per logical qubit, compared with 3.0 for memory alone. The architecture thus preserves the resource advantage of ultra-high-rate quantum memory while enabling universal logical computation, providing a route towards low-overhead fault-tolerant quantum computers.

### Abstract

Continuous-variable quantum information processing with mechanical oscillators requires a uni- versal gate set, including a genuine non-Gaussian evolution. While circuit quantum acoustodynamics (cQAD) experiments have realized bilinear and quartic (four-wave mixing) operations by coupling mechanical modes to transmon qubits, the intrinsic Kerr nonlinearity of these devices precludes access to odd-order effective Hamiltonians such as trisqueezing. ### Abstract

We revisit the effective radial Schrödinger equation for a charged spinless particle in collinear static electric and magnetic fields that was formulated in our earlier work within the tridiagonal representation approach (TRA). In that model the angular factor of the electric coupling is replaced by the orientation parameter $C=\cosθ$, the linear Zeeman term is diagonal in the azimuthal quantum number, and the effective field-modified central potential takes the form $V(r)=\Vt(r)+qECr$. Here we show that the same equation can be solved exactly without the TRA, by the Nikiforov--Uvarov (NU) method, once the potential component $\Vt(r)$ is prescribed. Three examples are worked out in closed form: a Kratzer-type component, which yields a linear-plus-Coulomb-plus-inverse-square central potential; a pseudoharmonic-type component, which yields a linear-plus-harmonic-plus-inverse-square potential; and a Hulthén-type component with a repulsive core (Manning--Rosen form), which yields a screened confining potential with a finite number of $s$-wave bound states. For each case we obtain the energy spectrum, the normalized eigenfunctions in terms of Laguerre or Jacobi polynomials, the location of the potential minimum, and closed-form radial moments. The spectra are independent of the orientation $θ$ and of the electric field, in agreement with the structure found by the TRA, while the field enters through the shape of $V(r)$, the radial moments, and the expectation value $\langle V\rangle_k$. All analytical results are confirmed by an independent finite-difference diagonalization.

### Abstract

We study how readout errors affect dynamics in monitored quantum circuits. Such errors make postselection effectively incomplete, conditioning trajectories on imperfect rather than true records. Focusing on U(1)-symmetric circuits, we find that the charge-sharpening transition survives at a finite error rate, with the sharp phase's vanishing fluctuations replaced by a finite steady-state plateau. The charge fluctuations show a shallow minimum near the ideal transition point as a function of measurement rate, signaling the competition between measurement-induced purification and noise-induced mixing. Our results show that charge sharpening survives readout errors in a quantitative and experimentally accessible way.
